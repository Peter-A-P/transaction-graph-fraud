Launderers move money as a pattern: fanning in from many accounts to one, scattering out and gathering again, cycling back to where it started. One payment at a time, none of it looks unusual. This project put three ways of scoring on the same accounts, in the same time-ordered split, and measured what each one catches: the row alone, the row plus features from each account's neighbours, and a graph neural network, with a foundation model beside them that is never trained on this data.

The basics, in plain words

Three ideas carry the rest of this page. If you build fraud models for a living, skip ahead to the ring.

A transaction graph

Every account is a point and every payment is an arrow between two points. An account's neighbours are the accounts it paid or was paid by: one hop. Their neighbours are two hops. A laundering ring is a small pattern of arrows that looks ordinary one arrow at a time.

Three ways of scoring, and a fourth

R gradient-boosted trees on the account's own row. G the same trees, given the row plus counts and amounts summed over its neighbours. N a graph neural network that passes messages along the arrows. TabFM Google's tabular foundation model, shown a sample of the training rows that keeps every fraud case, and asked to score with no training.

An alert queue, and AUPRC

A bank can investigate only so many accounts, so the useful question is how much of the fraud lands in the top 1% of scores: the alert queue. Precision-recall AUC (AUPRC) sums up the whole ranking in one number, from 0 to 1; with fraud this rare, a random guess scores about the share of fraud, a few percent.

The three datasets, and why each one is here

Elliptic real

203,769 Bitcoin transactions over 49 time steps, about 4,500 labelled illicit by Elliptic. Its features already summarise each transaction's neighbours, which makes it a hard test for anything that adds the graph again.

Elliptic++ real

The same network seen as 822,942 wallets and the payments between them, used with the authors' written permission. Its published wallet features summarise each wallet's whole life, so they were rebuilt step by step here, so that no wallet is scored with knowledge of its future.

IBM AML synthetic

Twelve million bank transfers between 1.2 million accounts, from IBM's generator, which plants eight named laundering shapes and records which transfers are laundering. The only set where recall can be measured by shape, and the only one drawn here.

How every number was made honest
  1. 1Split by time, never at random. Train on the past, test on later accounts the model has never seen, the way a bank meets them.
  2. 2No feature reads a label. A registry refuses any neighbourhood feature that touches a fraud label, and a leakage test runs on every build.
  3. 3The same items for every arm. One evaluator reads every model's scores without knowing which model wrote them, and refuses to compare runs that scored different items.
  4. 4Choose on validation. The hop count and the network design shown for each arm are the ones with the best validation score, never the best test score.
  5. 5Intervals over groups. Resample time steps or hours, not accounts, because accounts in the same hour are not independent.
  6. 6Time it on a named machine. Latency comes from a rented server with its price and date, never from a laptop.

When the world changes overnight

At Elliptic's time step 43 a large dark market was shut down, and illicit Bitcoin traffic changed shape. Every model was trained on steps 1 to 34 and then frozen. Each line runs from its score before the shutdown to its score after it. The dashed lines are allowed to recover: before each test step, the row model is retrained, or the foundation model's examples are swapped, on the four latest labelled steps.

The number most papers report is not the one a bank would get

Most published results on these datasets split accounts at random, so the model trains on the same days it is tested on. This project's split is by time. Each row runs from the honest score to the random-split score of the same model.

Can an investigator read why it flagged?

A flag an investigator cannot explain is a flag they cannot act on. For every flagged transfer, each neighbouring account is removed in turn and the transfer rescored through the exported model; the neighbours whose removal drops the score most are the explanation. The test: how often do those top neighbours include an account the generator actually used for laundering, against the same number of neighbours picked at random?

Would it pass a release gate?

Project 03's release gate decides whether a challenger is at least as good as the model it would replace, within a stated margin, before anyone ships it. Here the question is the business one: of the laundering in the test period, how much more does the 1% alert queue reach than the row model's, allowing a 3-point margin?

What a live score costs

A score behind a payment decision has a few dozen milliseconds. One call here scores one transfer from scratch: both accounts' neighbourhoods fetched from the store, the graph features computed by the batch run's own code, and the score from the exported model. A thousand calls on a rented server. The bar is the median; the whisker reaches the 99th percentile.

What to take from it

These hold for four datasets and these model versions. Use them as the questions to ask before funding a graph.

Start with graph features, not a graph neural network

Neighbourhood counts and sums fed to the gradient-boosted trees a team already runs gave the gain on every dataset. The graph neural network never beat them, cost GPU time to train, and moved more from one random seed to the next.

One hop, from counters, is the live design

On the bank transfers the second hop added nothing measurable and cost several times as much to fetch. One hop, with each account's own counts kept as running counters, scores in milliseconds in every store tested.

Ask how the split was made

A random split hides the drift a bank lives with and can make a weak model look excellent. Any graph fraud result should be tested on accounts from a later period than the ones it learned from.

When the market shifts, fresh labels are what recover

When the market changed, every frozen model collapsed, graph or not. What recovered was fresh labels: retraining on the latest weeks, or swapping the foundation model's examples, which costs no training at all.

How this was built

I designed this project and made the calls in it. I wrote the plan and drew the three diagrams on the architecture page before the first line of code, and wherever the build proved the plan wrong I amended it in place, with the date and the reason. The leakage test came before the first feature: it refuses any test-period row in a training table and any feature that reads a fraud label. I chose to split every dataset by time and to run the usual random split once, only to publish the gap it opens, because that gap is why published graph fraud results look better than anything a bank would see. My own build had a leak of the same kind: graph features computed with sight of later days scored higher than the honest version. It was fixed once found, and a leakage test now runs on every build. I measured the business question as an alert queue, how much of the laundering a fixed team reaches, because that is the number that decides whether a graph is worth funding.

I used an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) for implementation and drafting, which is now standard practice in software, data science and machine learning work. Every number on this page is read from data.json, which the repository's site command writes from its committed results, and every one can be reproduced from the repository.

The alert-queue view and the ONNX scorer interface come from my real-time fraud platform (project 09), and the paired comparisons run through my release gate (project 03). The data is public: Elliptic under its licence and never redistributed, Elliptic++ with its authors' written permission, and IBM's synthetic transfers, the only accounts drawn here.

Glossary
Alert queue
The accounts a team has time to investigate, here the top 1% of scores. Recall in the queue is how much of the laundering it reaches.
AUPRC (precision-recall AUC)
One number, from 0 to 1, for how well a model ranks fraud above everything else. A random guess scores about the share of fraud, a few percent here.
Hop
One step along the payments graph. An account's one-hop neighbours are the accounts it paid or was paid by; two hops are their neighbours.
Graph features
Counts and amounts summed over an account's neighbours, such as how many accounts paid it, given to the same gradient-boosted trees as the row.
Graph neural network (GraphSAGE, GAT)
A model that learns by passing messages along the payments between accounts, instead of being handed summaries of them.
Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost)
Many small decision trees, each correcting the last. The model most fraud teams already run.
Foundation model (TabFM)
A model pretrained elsewhere that scores a new table from a sample of labelled rows shown to it, with no training on this data.
Time-ordered split
Training on earlier accounts and testing on later ones, the way a bank meets them. A random split mixes the two and lets the model learn from the period it is tested on.
Leakage
A model seeing information it would not have when it decides, such as later days or a fraud label. It looks good in testing and fails in use.
Confidence interval
The range a measured number would likely fall in if the measurement were repeated. Here it is a 95% bootstrap over time steps or hours, never over single accounts.
Release gate and margin
A check that a new model is at least as good as the one it would replace, allowing a stated margin (3 points here), before anyone ships it.
ONNX
A standard file format for trained models, so a model trained in one tool can be served by another.
p50, p99
The time within which half, and 99 in 100, of the live scores finished.
